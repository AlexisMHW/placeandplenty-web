There is a window for Friendsgiving. It opens once the weather turns and
it closes the moment everyone's calendar fills up with the real holidays.

Pick the date first. Everything else is easier than you think.

## The Friendsgiving plan at a glance

- **Four to five weeks before:** choose the date and send the invitation.
- **Three weeks before:** set the contribution slots and ask for dietary needs.
- **Two weeks before:** confirm the guest count, menu and seating plan.
- **One week before:** fill any menu gaps and buy drinks, paper goods and shelf-stable food.
- **The day before:** clear the kitchen, set the table, chill drinks and pull out serving dishes.
- **The final hour:** finish the food, light the room and stop adding things.

## Send the date before the menu exists

The most common way Friendsgiving dies is waiting until you have a plan
before you invite anyone. By then it is the 15th and four people are
already travelling.

Send the date. You can figure out the food later — and if you are doing
it as a potluck, you were always going to figure it out with them.

## Ask for dishes, not "a side"

"Bring a side" gets you four potato dishes and no vegetable. Ask for
specific slots and people will happily fill them:

- Two vegetables, one of them green
- One starch that is not the potatoes
- Bread
- Two desserts
- Ice, wine, something non-alcoholic

Specific requests are also easier to say yes to. "Bring whatever" is a
decision you have handed to someone else; "can you bring a green
vegetable for eight" is a task.

### A balanced contribution list for 12

- Host: main dish, gravy or sauce, and one dependable starch
- Guest 1: green vegetable for 12
- Guest 2: second vegetable or salad for 12
- Guest 3: bread or rolls
- Guest 4: dessert
- Guest 5: second dessert or fruit
- Guest 6: ice and non-alcoholic drinks
- Guest 7: wine, beer or a second drink option if appropriate

The host should keep the food that controls the oven and the timing. Give away the dishes that travel well and can wait at room temperature.

## What the host should keep

Keep the things that are hard to transport and the things that set the
timing: the turkey or whatever is standing in for it, the gravy, and
anything that has to come out of your oven hot.

Give away everything that travels well and everything that is fine at
room temperature.

## Oven maths

This is the actual constraint and it is worth doing on paper. You have
one oven. If four people arrive with dishes that each "just need twenty
minutes at 350", you have eighty minutes of oven and a cold dinner.

Two rules fix it:

1. Tell people whether their dish can arrive needing the oven. Most can
   be told "bring it ready to eat or ready to be room temperature."
2. Whatever comes out of your oven last should be the thing that matters
   most. Everything else can wait on the counter under foil.

## Seating: more people than chairs

You will have more people than chairs. This is normal and it is not a
problem to solve with furniture.

Two tables that do not match are better than one table that seats
everybody but nobody can reach across. A card table with a cloth over it
is a table. Someone will end up on the piano bench and it will be the
best seat.

Count plates and forks, not chairs. That is the number that stops the
evening.

## The part worth protecting

Somewhere in the middle of all this, everyone is going to be in one room
at the same time, which basically never happens any more.

Do not spend it in the kitchen. That is the whole reason for the date.

## Send a little of the gathering home

If you want a parting gift, plan extra portions of a favorite dish or dessert
and include nice take-home containers in the budget. For food contributed by
guests, ask before promising any of it for take-home boxes. Read Alexis’s
[food-instead-of-party-favors approach](/coordinated-host/food-instead-of-party-favors)
for portion planning, packaging, and a thoughtful send-off.
