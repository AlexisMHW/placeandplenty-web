At some point in the next month, there is a Saturday that is still warm
enough to eat outside, and then there is not another one until spring.

That is the whole reason. It does not need to be anyone's birthday.

## One thing off the fire, everything else already done

Cooking outdoors for a group goes wrong when the grill becomes the
kitchen. Give it one job — one protein, or one big vegetable, or flatbread
— and make everything else in advance.

A dinner that works:

- Something warm off the grill
- A grain salad that is better after an hour
- Something green and sharp to cut it
- Bread
- One dessert you did not make

That is a complete table, and only one item is on a clock.

## The part everyone forgets: it gets dark

Early fall dark arrives fast and about ninety minutes earlier than your
body expects. Two things to sort before anyone arrives:

**Light.** Not overhead light — nobody wants to eat under a floodlight.
Anything low and warm: string lights, candles in jars, a lamp run out
through a window. Enough to see the food.

**Warmth.** The temperature drops with the light. A stack of blankets on
a chair by the door is the single highest-return thing you can do, and
people remember it for years. A fire pit if you have one, but blankets
alone are enough.

## Seating that admits what it is

Nobody has eight matching outdoor chairs. Bring the kitchen chairs out.
Put a cushion on the bench. Someone will sit on the step and be perfectly
happy.

The goal is that everyone can put a plate down somewhere — not that the
furniture matches.

## A plan for weather

Decide the indoor version now, while it is sunny and easy to be
optimistic. Where does the food go, where do people sit, and what is the
cutoff time to make the call.

You will probably not need it. Deciding it in advance costs nothing, and
deciding it at 4pm in the rain costs the evening.
