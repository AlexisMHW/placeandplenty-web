The exhausting part of RSVPs is not the number of people. It is the number of places their answers can hide.

One person replies through the invitation. One texts your spouse. Someone tells you at school pickup. An aunt comments “can’t wait” on a photo but never technically says whether she is coming.

By the deadline, the host is doing archaeology.

## Keep one canonical guest list

Every response has to land in one place, even if the guest answered somewhere else.

That list should show:

- invited;
- yes;
- maybe;
- no;
- no response;
- who within a household is attending;
- plus-ones;
- dietary notes;
- promised contributions.

If the food count lives in one spreadsheet and the names live in an invitation app, neither is the real list.

## Give people a real deadline

“Let me know” sounds friendly and creates no urgency. Use a date and explain why it matters without writing an essay.

> Please respond by October 3 so we can plan the table and food for everyone.

That is warm, clear and useful.

## Send one reminder before the deadline

The best reminder is short and contains the response link again.

> A quick reminder to RSVP for Saturday by October 3. We’re planning the food this weekend and would love to know whether you can make it.

Do not send a reminder that makes people search for the original invitation. Do not make the message sound like a debt-collection notice.

## Follow up according to consequence

Not every missing answer deserves the same amount of energy.

Follow up individually when:

- the person is immediate family or part of the occasion;
- the household adds several people to the count;
- they agreed to bring an important dish;
- seating, tickets, rentals or food depend on the answer;
- you genuinely need to know whether they are safe or supported getting there.

For a casual guest whose answer changes nothing important, the deadline can stand. No response can mean not included in the final count.

## Keep households together

People often decide as a household, not as independent database rows.

The response should make clear:

- which adults are coming;
- which children are coming;
- whether a named guest is bringing a partner;
- whether the response covers everyone invited.

This avoids the classic “I said yes, but I meant all five of us” surprise.

## Do not count maybes as guaranteed food

For seating, you can keep flexible room for maybes. For expensive food, do not shop as if every maybe is a yes.

A workable approach is:

- buy shelf-stable essentials with a modest buffer;
- wait on highly perishable or costly items until the count firms up;
- keep one flexible dish that can stretch;
- make sure drinks and ice have enough room for late changes.

## Close the loop

After the deadline:

1. Record any final direct replies in the canonical list.
2. Confirm the people bringing something important.
3. Freeze the working food and seating count.
4. Stop reopening the entire plan for every late message.

You can still welcome someone kindly without allowing every delayed answer to reorganize the week.

## The principle

An RSVP system should reduce uncertainty. If it creates another inbox for the host to monitor and another list to reconcile, it has moved the work—not solved it.
