The instinct when you are nervous about feeding people is to add another
dish. It feels like generosity. It is usually the thing that turns a
good evening into a stressful one, because every extra dish is a pan, a
timing, a serving bowl, a space on the table, and a decision you have to
make while people are already in your house.

## The rule

**Sitting down:** one main, two sides, and one of either a starter or a
pudding. Four things. If you want both ends of the meal, buy one of them
— a good cheese, a decent tart — rather than cooking it.

**Standing up:** five or six things in total, and that number does not
change when the headcount does. Eighteen people do not need eleven
dishes. They need bigger quantities of the same six.

That second point is the one people get wrong most often. Variety scales
with the length of the evening, not with the number of guests. Add
quantity, not variety.

## Why more dishes makes it worse

Every dish you add costs you in four places at once:

- **Oven and hob space**, which is the real constraint in almost every
  home kitchen and the one nobody counts until the day.
- **A serving vessel**, which is where the "I need to buy more bowls"
  spiral starts.
- **Table space**, which is finite, and which food is competing for with
  glasses, elbows and anything decorative you had planned.
- **Your attention in the last forty minutes**, which is the only
  genuinely scarce resource of the evening.

Four dishes cooked calmly beats seven cooked at a run, and guests cannot
tell how much effort went in — only how the room feels when they arrive.

## The one dish that earns its place

If you add anything beyond the rule, make it something finished before
anyone arrives and served cold or at room temperature. A salad you dress
at the last second does not count; a bowl of marinated something that
has been in the fridge since morning does. It adds generosity to the
table without adding anything to the last hour.

## When a potluck changes the maths

If people are bringing things, the rule applies to the *whole table*,
not to your share. Six things total — and if four people are arriving
with something, you are cooking two. The common mistake is to plan a
full menu of your own and treat contributions as extra, which is how a
table ends up with three potato dishes and no vegetables.

Ask for slots rather than for "a side" and the count stays honest.
