Every host has done this. You arrange the living room properly. Cushions
straightened, chairs angled, a surface cleared for glasses. Then eleven
people arrive and stand shoulder to shoulder in a kitchen the size of a
lift, holding drinks, while the living room sits empty and beautifully
lit.

It is not a failure of hosting. It is where the drinks are.

## People stand where they can put something down

That is nearly the whole rule. A guest holding a glass and a small plate
has no hands left, so they will drift until they find a surface at
elbow height, and then they will stay there. If the only such surface is
your kitchen counter, everyone will be at your kitchen counter, standing
between you and the oven.

So the question is not "how do I get people out of the kitchen". It is
"where else can someone put a glass down".

## Three moves that do most of the work

**Move the drinks away from the food.** Not to another floor — just to
the far end of whatever space you have. A side table, a sideboard, the
end of a bookshelf. Drinks are the anchor; wherever they are is where
the room forms. If drinks and food occupy the same square metre, so will
your guests.

**Give the kitchen one deliberate leaning surface.** Trying to keep
people out of the kitchen never works, so pick where in it you want them
and clear that spot properly. The end of the island furthest from the
hob. A stretch of counter with nothing on it. If you leave it ambiguous,
people will choose the spot directly in front of the drawer with the
serving spoons in it.

**Check what is behind people.** The bin, the fridge, the cupboard with
the glasses, the door to the loo. Every one of those is a place someone
will need to get to roughly forty times, and if the natural standing
spot blocks one of them you will spend the evening saying "sorry, just
squeezing past".

## The bit nobody plans

Coats and bags. They go somewhere, and if you have not decided where,
that place will be the chair you needed. A bed in a room with the light
on is fine. A hook by the door is better. Deciding in advance takes
four seconds and saves the small indignity of moving a stranger's coat
off a dining chair while they watch.

## A note on seating

For anything under about eight people, you do not need a chair each in
the standing part of the evening — you need somewhere for roughly half
of them to sit and a clear route between the two ends of the room. A
room that is 60% full feels lively. A room that is 90% full feels like a
queue.

If you are sitting down to eat, that is a different problem and a
counted one: chairs, place settings, elbow room. But the hour before
that is not a seating problem. It is a surfaces-and-drinks problem, and
it is the hour that sets how the whole evening feels.
