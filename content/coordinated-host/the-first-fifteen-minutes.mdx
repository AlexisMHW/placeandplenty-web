The opening of a gathering sets its temperature, and it is almost
entirely decided by whether the host is available.

Not calm. Not finished. *Available* — able to look at someone, take
their coat, put a drink in their hand and say something that is not
"sorry, two minutes". Everything below exists to buy you that.

## Three decisions, made the day before

**What goes in their hand in the first thirty seconds.** One thing, the
same for everyone, already open or already poured. Not a question, not a
choice, not something that requires opening a bottle while holding a
conversation. A choice is a lovely thing to offer at minute ten. At
minute one it is a small task you have handed to a guest who is still
standing in their coat.

**Where the coats go.** Decide it, and say it out loud as they arrive.
An undecided answer produces a pile on a chair you needed.

**What is already out.** Something on the table before the doorbell —
olives, crisps, nuts, anything. Its actual purpose is not food. It is to
give a room something to gather around before there is enough of a room
to gather. A table with nothing on it makes early guests stand at the
edges.

## The thing to stop doing

Do not be finishing a dish when the first person arrives. If a dish
cannot be finished before people come, it should be one that is finished
*well* after they arrive — during the settled part of the evening, in a
kitchen that already has people in it and a conversation going.

The awkward zone is the twenty minutes either side of the doorbell.
Nothing that requires your full attention should be scheduled there. It
is worth moving a course, changing a recipe or buying the pudding to
protect it.

## Early guests are not a problem

Someone will arrive eight minutes early. This is not rudeness; it is a
person who did not want to be late. The answer is the same three
decisions above — a drink, a place for the coat, something on the table
— and then genuinely letting them be early. Handing an early guest a
job is one of the warmest things you can do, provided the job is real
and small: opening wine, lighting candles, finding the serving spoons.

## The note itself

You spent the week on the food. The part your guests will describe
afterwards is the first ninety seconds, and none of it is cooking.

The preparation serves the people. The people are the point.
